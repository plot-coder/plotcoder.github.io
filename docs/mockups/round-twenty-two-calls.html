Round twenty-two's four calls, before and after

Four things the round on "The Last Bus" left for Robert (2026-09-20). Each is drawn the way the app behaves today, on the left, beside one or two ways it could behave, using the round's own wall. Under each: what it costs, what I would do, and what a yes means. Nothing here is built. They are in the order I would take them; each stands alone, so a yes to one commits to none of the others.

1 · A card that is on the wall and not in the film

What happened. The writer had the breakdown two ways, chose the second, and said "keep the other; I may come back to it… it stays on the wall where I can see it, but it is not in the film." The app has two states for a card: in the story, or tucked behind another as "not chosen". So the kept card went back into the film: counted as a page, printed in the script as a scene, and — having no arrows — slotted into the middle of the story by where it happened to sit. The agent labelled it "not in the film" using the card's open words, which is a misuse. (Entries 47, 50, 51, 64, 85.)

A third state for any card, not only a version: a scene the writer cuts but will not throw away, an idea with nowhere to go yet. Faded, no number, anywhere on the wall, out of the order, the count, the pages and the exports; the reading lists it and asks nothing. choose_version with keep would set the kept card aside.

What I would do: A now, and B when a round asks for it. A answers exactly what the writer said, with a word added to a record that exists; it cannot clutter a wall, because a kept card is always behind the scene it was a version of. B is the truer picture of how writers work — every writer has a pile of cut scenes — but it is a new state every part of the app must learn, and only one round has asked, about a version. What A cannot say: a scene cut outright, with no chosen version to stand behind, still has to be deleted or left in the film. If you already know you want a cut pile, say B and skip A: B covers both.
A yes to A means: choose_version with keep leaves the other card tucked behind, marked kept; the wall draws the pair with "decided" over it; the reading has a "versions kept" line; choosing the kept card swaps them. A yes to B means: a new "set aside" gesture on a card's menu and a set_aside tool, a faded card with no number, and a line in the reading.

2 · An open card is all or nothing, so an honest wall asks almost nothing

What happened. The writer knew seven scenes and no change line for any of them: "I don't know yet — leave it open." A card will not be created without a change line unless the whole card is marked open, and a whole-card open switches off every question about that card: its place, its cast, its arrows, its fold. So every card on the wall was open, the wall asked one question, and its reading — "checked and clean", fourteen times over — made a nearly empty wall look nearly finished. The timetable's fold had no payoff and nothing asked. (Entries 15, 17, 23, 28, 42, 86.)

B · and the reading leads with what is open

read_wall:
  this wall: 1 question asked · 17 things left open by the writer's word · 9 of 10 scenes unwritten
  board: "Board 1" — its name is open …

One line at the head of the reading and of list_board, so nobody — agent or writer — takes a quiet wall for a finished one. It claims nothing (D21): three counts, no verdict. It can ship with A or without it.

Not drawn, on purpose: making the open words a list of separate things, each closed on its own. It would answer the retyping (39, 79) but it turns a sentence of the writer's into a form, and A removes most of the reason a card carries three clauses at once.

What I would do: A and B together. This is the finding I would fix first if I could only fix one: it is the difference between a wall that helps a writer with a half-formed idea and one that goes silent on them, and a half-formed idea is what the app is for. What it costs: a field, a word on create_note and update_note, a line in the reading, and an offer on the card's change line like the one on a place. What it cannot say: a card with a change line open and everything else blank will now be asked four questions at once, where today it is asked none; on a first pass that is loud. The reading already groups such questions ("5 cards say no place"), so it is a few lines, not thirty.
A yes means: changeOpen on a card, by the writer's word; the card shows the words in the change line's place; create_note accepts it instead of a change line; the reading lists it in one grouped line and goes on asking the card's other questions; and the reading opens with the three counts.

3 · A place left open prints the writer's words as the scene heading

What happened. The writer left the first morning's place open with the words "I don't know yet". The field is for the writer's words about why there is no place, and the heading prints them after the mark — so the script's first scene is headed PLACE NOT DECIDED: I DON'T KNOW YET - SEPTEMBER, MORNING. Words that read well on a card's edge read as nonsense where a slugline goes. (Entry 70.) Yesterday's change for a card with no place — NO PLACE YET and then the headline — already reads better than this.

The same shape as NO PLACE YET: the mark says why there is no slugline, the headline says which scene, every heading is its own. The writer's words go in a note under the heading — Fountain's own form for a note, which does not print in a finished script — and come back in as the open place's words.

What I would do: A. It is one function again, the one changed yesterday, and the import's reading of the note. What it costs: the writer's words leave the heading line; on the app's page they show as a grey note under it. What it cannot say: nothing new — it says the same two things as today, in places where each reads as what it is.
A yes means: a scene whose place is open is headed PLACE NOT DECIDED: and its headline; the writer's words for why sit on the next line as a note, in the app, in print and in every export.

4 · A fact about the whole film — drawn before the round, now answered by it

What happened. The notes say the film runs September to New Year and the cut is announced in the first scene. Nobody told the agent where such a thing goes. It put both in the premise, saying as it did that the premise "is the nearest, and it isn't what a premise is". Then, writing the first morning, it found the other half: the premise said the cut is announced in this scene and the scene's card said nothing, so "an agent writing only from the card would never know". And read_wall prints a premise only when it is open, never when it is set. (Entries 13, 24, 74; round twenty 9 and 20 before them.)

A · say what the premise is for, and read it back where a scene is written

set_premise — "… Also where a fact about the whole film goes when no card holds it: its span ("September to New Year"), a rule it keeps ("Con does not die in this film"). A fact about one scene belongs on its card."
read_wall:
  premise: "A country bus route in the west… September to New Year; the cut is announced in September, in the first scene."
  logline: open …
read_pages, at the head, above scene 1:
  the film (the premise): "… the cut is announced in September, in the first scene."

The same sentence in the agent's guide and the writer's guide. No new field: a span worked out from the cards' whens would be the app making a claim, and a list of "rules" would be the premise under another name.

What I would do: A, which is yesterday's drawing plus the last line — the premise at the head of the pages, which the round showed is where a scene's writer needs it. What it cannot say: the premise is prose, so the wall cannot check a scene against "the cut is announced in the first scene"; that stays a reading for a person or an agent. A fact that belongs to one scene ("the cut is announced here") is still better on that scene's card, and the sentence says so.
A yes means: three sentences of words, one line in read_wall, one line at the head of read_pages. Nothing a person sees in the app changes.
